# Pattern catalog

Every pattern is one form of the same habit: a model picks the choice that fits the widest range of readers and subjects, while a human picks for one reader and one subject. Structural habits outlast word habits, so they come first.

**Strength.** Patterns in A act on one sighting. Patterns marked *weak alone* need company from other tells in the same passage before you edit. A careful writer makes any of these on purpose sometimes, so several tells together are the real signal.

**Scale.** Look at paragraph shape as well as sentences. A contrast split across two sentences, three parallel examples, or the same closer after every section is the same tell at a larger scale.

---

## A. Staging instead of stating

The strongest and most frequent tells. Act on one sighting.

### 1. Not X but Y (binary contrasts, negative listing)

**Watch for:** not X but Y; not just / not only / not merely X, but Y; it's not X, it's Y; X rather than Y; the question isn't X, it's Y; X isn't the problem, Y is; it feels like X, it's actually Y; stops being X and starts being Y; not because X, because Y; the contrast split across sentences ("This does not mean X. It means Y."); negative listing ("Not a X. Not a Y. A Z."); a clipped negative tail ("..., no guessing"); the positive twin "Most people do X. The smart ones do Y."
**Problem:** The negative half names something nobody claimed so the positive half sounds bigger. State Y directly. Keep a contrast only when the negative half corrects a belief the reader actually holds, or both halves carry information.
**Before:** It's not just about the beat riding under the vocals; it's part of the aggression and atmosphere. It's not merely a song, it's a statement.
**After:** The heavy beat adds to the aggressive tone.
**Before (split):** The question isn't the model. It's the eval.
**After:** The eval matters more than the model.
**Before (clipped tail):** The options come from the selected item, no guessing.
**After:** The options come from the selected item, so the user doesn't have to guess.

### 2. One-line closers, dramatic fragments, fake-profound kickers

**Watch for:** a one-sentence paragraph that restates the one before it; "That is the real win." "That distinction matters." "Read that again." "Let that sink in." "Full stop." "Make no mistake."; the same closer after several sections; a line after an example that names what it showed ("This shows the importance of...", "The message was clear:"); a row of fragments ("No aesthetic prior. No nostalgia."); "X. And Y. And Z."; "That's it. That's the whole thing."; ALL CAPS or every. single. day.; a final aphorism or mic-drop metaphor; every paragraph ending on a punchy one-liner.
**Problem:** The line asks the reader to pause on a claim instead of adding to it. Cut a closer that repeats. Do not rewrite a kicker into a better metaphor: delete it and end on the clearest concrete sentence already there. Merge a row of fragments into one sentence with a specific claim. One short sentence is fine when it carries a new fact.
**Before:** Then AlphaEvolve arrived. It had no preference for symmetry. No aesthetic prior. No nostalgia for human taste. The old rules were gone.
**After:** AlphaEvolve changed the search because it did not favor symmetry or human-looking designs, which made some older assumptions less useful.

### 3. Sayings that sound deep (quotables)

**Watch for:** the real question is, at its core, in reality, what really matters, fundamentally, the deeper issue, the heart of the matter, the uncomfortable truth is, X is the Y of Z, X becomes a trap, X is not a tool but a mirror, the language/currency/architecture of, X is a feature not a bug; any line that reads like a pull-quote.
**Problem:** An ordinary point dressed as a hidden truth. Replace the saying with the specific claim.
**Before:** Symmetry is the language of trust. Efficiency becomes a trap when teams forget the human layer.
**After:** Symmetric layouts often feel more predictable to users. Teams can over-optimize workflows and miss how people actually use them.

### 4. Staged run-up before the point (throat-clearing, faux insight, rhetorical setups, colon reveals)

**Watch for:**
- Announcements: Let's dive in, let's explore, let's break this down, here's what you need to know, now let's look at, without further ado, heads up, quick note, let me walk you through, I want to explore.
- Staged candor: Here's the thing, Here's what I mean, Here's why, The thing is, Let me be clear, I'll be honest, Let's be honest, Real talk, Honestly? / Look, as a standalone opener, It turns out, The truth is.
- Faux insight: This is the part most people skip, What most people get wrong, Here's what nobody tells you, The part everyone misses.
- Rhetorical setups (a trailing "Think about it." is a §2 closer): What if I told you..., Think about it:, Plot twist:, Spoiler:, Hint:, a question the next sentence answers ("Is it worth it? It depends.").
- Colon reveals: a noun phrase, a colon, then a dramatic lowercase reveal ("The best part: it learns." "The detail that makes it work: a separate agent grades it.").
**Problem:** The writer announces the point, or stages candor or expertise, instead of making the point. Remove the run-up and state the claim. "Honestly" or "look" inside a casual sentence is ordinary; the tell is the standalone opener before a routine claim. Use colons for lists, labels and quotes, not fake drama, and prefer sentence case after a colon.
**Before:** The part everyone misses: distribution is the real moat.
**After:** Distribution is the moat.
**Before:** Is it worth the price? Honestly? It depends on how often you'll use it.
**After:** Whether it's worth the price depends on how often you'll use it.

### 5. Arguing with no one

**Watch for:** This isn't (mainly) about, I'm not saying, To be clear, Don't get me wrong, This is not to say, Some might say... but, A tempting approach would be, One might be tempted to, You might think... but, It would be easy to just, And that's okay.
**Problem:** The text answers an objection or rejects an option that appears nowhere else, or grants permission nobody asked for. Remove the defense; if it holds a real claim, state the claim. Keep an objection the text attributes or answers in full.
**Before:** Session tokens are rotated every 24 hours. A tempting approach would be to rotate them by restarting the auth service on a cron job, but that would drop every active session. Rotation happens in place, and clients refresh transparently.
**After:** Session tokens are rotated every 24 hours, in place, and clients refresh transparently.

## B. Rhythm by rule

Shapes and punctuation applied everywhere, whether the meaning asks for them or not.

### 6. Forced triads

**Problem:** Ideas arrive in threes to sound complete. Can be one sentence ("innovation, inspiration, and insights"), three parallel examples, or three short facts and a lesson. Check each item adds a distinct idea. Two items often beat three. Keep three when the meaning has three parts.
**Before:** The event features keynote sessions, panel discussions, and networking opportunities. Attendees can expect innovation, inspiration, and industry insights.
**After:** The event includes talks and panels, with time for informal networking between sessions.

### 7. Robotic rhythm and repeated openings

**Watch for:** three or more sentences of the same length in a row; identical paragraph shapes; several sentences opening with the same subject ("She noted... She noted... She filed..."); stacked punchy fragments; paragraphs that all start with "So"; habitual Wh- openers ("What makes this hard is...", "Why this matters:").
**Problem:** Repetition handled by rule, not by ear. Merge sentences, change the subject, start with the action, or name the thing ("What makes this hard is X" becomes "The constraint is X"). Do not ban a word outright, and keep repetition the writer uses on purpose ("She came. She saw. She conquered.").
**Before:** She noted the door. She noted the lock on it. She filed both away.
**After:** She noted the door and its lock, then filed both away.

### 8. Dashes as the universal connector

**Default:** Short copy (posts, emails, bios, cover letters, commit messages): no em dashes (—) or en dashes (–) used as punctuation. Longer drafts: at most 1 or 2, only where they clearly beat a comma, period, colon or parentheses. Double hyphens (` -- `) count. If the writer's sample, or their own clearly human writing in the draft, uses dashes as a habit, keep about that rate. Leave dashes inside code, commands, paths, URLs and number ranges alone.
**Problem:** A dash lets the writer skip deciding how two clauses relate. One dash is *weak alone*; a text full of them is not.
**Before:** The new policy — announced without warning — affects thousands of workers.
**After:** The new policy, announced without warning, affects thousands of workers.

### 9. Stacked qualifiers

**Watch for:** to be fair, it's also possible, could potentially, might arguably, in some cases it may, it could be argued.
**Problem:** Qualifier after qualifier until every claim sounds unsure. Keep a hedge only when the doubt is real. Ordinary hedges like *perhaps*, *I think*, or *tends to* are human. *Weak alone.*
**Before:** It could potentially possibly be argued that the policy might have some effect on outcomes.
**After:** The policy may affect outcomes.

### 10. Hyphenated pairs everywhere

**Watch for:** high-quality, well-known, well-documented, long-term, real-time, client-facing after the noun.
**Problem:** Keep the hyphen before a noun ("a high-quality report"), drop it after ("the report is high quality"). Dictionary hyphenations (third-party, cross-functional) keep it everywhere. *Weak alone.*

### 11. Passive voice, missing subjects, false agency

**Watch for:** "No configuration file needed." "Mistakes were made." "It is believed that"; inanimate things doing human verbs: the decision emerges, a complaint becomes a fix, the culture shifts, the data tells us, the market rewards, the conversation moves toward.
**Problem:** The text hides who acts. Name the actor and put them first. If no specific person fits, "you" often works. Passive is fine when the actor truly doesn't matter. *Weak alone* for plain passive; false agency acts on one sighting.
**Before:** The complaint became a fix within a week.
**After:** The team fixed it within a week.

### 12. Synonym cycling

**Problem:** Rotating terms for variety makes the reader wonder if they are different things. If the clear word is right, repeat it.
**Before:** The agent reviews the draft. The assistant scores the piece. The tool suggests fixes.
**After:** The agent reviews the draft, scores it, and suggests fixes.

### 13. False ranges

**Problem:** "From X to Y" where X and Y aren't on a real scale.
**Before:** Our journey has taken us from the singularity of the Big Bang to the grand cosmic web, from the birth and death of stars to the enigmatic dance of dark matter.
**After:** The book covers the Big Bang, star formation, and current theories about dark matter.

## C. Inflation and borrowed authority

The fact underneath is usually fine. Keep it and remove the dressing.

### 14. Overused AI words

See `words.md`. These are tells wherever they appear, especially in clusters. A formal word outside that list is not a tell by itself.

### 15. Inflated significance and vague declaratives

**Watch for:** stands as a testament, a pivotal/crucial moment, plays a key/vital role, marks or shapes the, solidifies its position, underscores its importance, reflects a broader, enduring legacy, setting the stage for, evolving landscape, indelible mark; vague declaratives: "The reasons are structural." "The implications are significant." "The stakes are high." "This is genuinely hard." "This is what leadership actually looks like."
**Problem:** An ordinary detail is said to mark a change or matter deeply without naming how. Keep the fact, drop the significance, or name the specific implication.
**Before:** The launch marks a pivotal moment for the company.
**After:** The launch is the company's first paid product.

### 16. Stock sections and send-offs

**Watch for:** "Despite these challenges... continues to thrive", Challenges and Legacy, Future Outlook; "the future looks bright", "exciting times ahead", "a step in the right direction"; summary-recap endings: In conclusion, Ultimately, Overall, In summary, or a last paragraph that restates the piece.
**Problem:** The reader was just there. End on the last concrete point, takeaway or next action. If the source states real plans, use those.
**Before:** In conclusion, the future looks bright. Exciting times lie ahead as we continue this journey.
**After:** (Cut. End on the last concrete fact.)

### 17. Shallow -ing riders

**Watch for:** highlighting, underscoring, emphasizing, ensuring, reflecting, symbolizing, contributing to, cultivating, fostering, encompassing, showcasing.
**Problem:** An -ing phrase bolted onto a fact to make it sound deeper. Keep the fact; keep the rider only if the source supports it, or replace it with the real consequence.
**Before:** The launch adds file search, highlighting the team's commitment to better workflows.
**After:** The launch adds file search, so users can find old drafts without leaving the editor.

### 18. Sales language

**Watch for:** boasts, vibrant, rich (figurative), profound, exemplifies, commitment to, natural beauty, nestled, in the heart of, groundbreaking, renowned, diverse array, breathtaking, must-visit, stunning, seamless, game changer, this changes everything.
**Problem:** Reads like an ad. Say what the thing is.
**Before:** Nestled within the breathtaking region of Gonder, Alamata Raya Kobo stands as a vibrant town with a rich cultural heritage.
**After:** Alamata Raya Kobo is a town in the Gonder region of Ethiopia.

### 19. Borrowed authority and vague connection

**Watch for:** experts argue, studies show, industry reports suggest, many argue, widely regarded as, observers have cited; "featured in [list of outlets]", over N followers; associated with, linked to, tied to, in connection with.
**Problem:** An unnamed authority or a prestige list props up a claim, or a vague link hides the real relationship. Name the source and what it said, name the relationship, or cut it. Never invent a source; if the user has none, ask or flag it. A missing citation alone is not a tell.
**Before:** He is associated with the Rajhans Orchestra, which he founded and conducts.
**After:** He founded and conducts the Rajhans Orchestra.

### 20. Avoiding is, are, has (fake-strong verbs)

**Watch for:** serves as, stands as, functions as, acts as, represents, marks; boasts, features, offers, maintains; weak verb phrases: made a decision, has the ability to, provides support for.
**Problem:** Simple verbs replaced with longer phrases. Use *is*, *are*, *has*, or the direct verb.
**Before:** Gallery 825 serves as LAAA's exhibition space. The gallery features four separate spaces and boasts over 3,000 square feet.
**After:** Gallery 825 is LAAA's exhibition space. It has four rooms totaling 3,000 square feet.

### 21. Narrator from a distance and lazy extremes

**Watch for:** "Nobody designed this." "People tend to..." "This is why..." lecturing from above the scene; sweeping every, always, never, everyone, nobody doing vague work.
**Problem:** Floats above the reader instead of putting them in the scene. "You" beats "people." Replace sweeping claims with the specific case. *Weak alone.*
**Before:** Nobody designed this. People just drift into it.
**After:** You don't sit down one day and decide to work weekends. It creeps in one Slack ping at a time.

## D. Formatting by rule

Templates also make clean formatting. The tell is decoration on every item.

### 22. Bold as decoration and labeled lists

**Problem:** Bold sprinkled mid-sentence, or every list item gets a bold label and a colon. Remove the bold. Turn a labeled list into prose when the labels add nothing. Use bullets only when two sentences of prose wouldn't read better.
**Before:**
> - **Performance:** Performance has been enhanced through optimized algorithms.
> - **Security:** Security has been strengthened with end-to-end encryption.
**After:** The update speeds up load times and adds end-to-end encryption.

### 23. Decorative headings

**Problem:** Title Case Headings, emojis or arrows on headings and bullets, a horizontal rule between every section, headers over two-sentence sections, a heading written for effect ("The decision, on one screen") instead of naming the content ("How the six options compare"). Use sentence case and drop the decoration.

### 24. Curly quotes

**Problem:** Curly quotes (“...”) where the target format uses straight quotes. Most editors auto-curl, so *weak alone*.

## E. Leftovers from the chat and the draft

Remove outright.

### 25. Chatbot residue and sycophancy

**Watch for:** I hope this helps, Of course!, Certainly!, Great question!, You're absolutely right, That's an excellent point, Would you like..., Want me to...?, let me know if, here is a...
**Problem:** The most certain tell in this list, and easy to miss when it wraps real content. Remove the wrapper, keep the content. A sign-off on a real letter or email is fine.

### 26. Knowledge-limit disclaimers and guesses

**Watch for:** as of [date], up to my last training update, while specific details are limited, based on available information, not widely documented, maintains a low profile, likely grew up/studied/began, it is believed that.
**Problem:** States where the model's knowledge ends, then fills the gap with a guess. Say what the source doesn't show, or cut the sentence.

### 27. Heading repeated in the first sentence

**Before:** "## Performance / Speed matters. / When users hit a slow page, they leave."
**After:** "## Performance / When users hit a slow page, they leave."

### 28. Writing about the document (metadiscourse)

**Watch for:** "The table below compares", "In this section, we'll", "As we'll see", "The rest of this essay explains", "As you can see", "The key point is", "This distinction matters", "That last part matters more than it sounds", redundant "In other words"; "generated from", "compiled from", "anything unconfirmed is flagged"; "was added to replace" outside a changelog.
**Problem:** The text talks about itself or tells the reader what to notice instead of showing it. Let facts and consequences carry the emphasis. Keep a source credit the reader can follow and a caveat that changes what they should do. *Weak alone* for a single description of the page.
**Before:** This function was added to replace the previous approach of iterating through all items, which caused O(n²) performance.
**After:** This function uses a hash map for O(1) lookups, avoiding the O(n²) cost of naive iteration.

## F. Writing for the wrong reader

### 29. Re-explaining what the reader knows

**Watch for:** a short reply that restates the problem, walks through the diagnosis and the evidence, and puts the decision in the last line; background the other person wrote or already agreed to.
**Problem:** In a reply the reader already has the context. Lead with the decision and keep only the reasoning that would change whether they agree: usually one fact they lack and any link they need to act. Act only when you can see the conversation or the text plainly is a reply.

---

## When not to act

- Inside a quotation, title, proper name, or a passage that discusses the phrase rather than uses it.
- Salutations and sign-offs on letters and comments.
- Text written before November 30, 2022 is not AI-written.
- Details that carry voice: a specific odd detail, mixed feelings, era-bound slang or in-jokes, a first-person choice, a real aside or self-correction, profanity, bluntness.
- Code blocks, inline code, commands, paths, YAML, data and link targets.
